What you’re talking about sounds like a **motion to waive or discharge remaining court costs**, often framed as a **“request for judicial review”** or “**application for remission of costs/fines**.” Here’s how it typically works, and it sounds like your court follows this general structure:

---

### ✅ Post-Graduation Court Cost Relief — How It Usually Works

**1. You get a formal “Congratulations” letter.**
From the Circuit Court Clerk or Judge. It’s symbolic, but also functional—it opens the door for follow-up actions like this.

**2. You initiate the process.**
Usually by contacting the court or your VTC coordinator and requesting to **be added to the docket** for a hearing to address fees. They don’t auto-schedule it; you have to ask.

**3. You fill out a short financial disclosure.**
It’s like a mini means-test form: income, living expenses, job status. Nothing crazy, but it gives the judge a paper trail showing your effort and financial picture.

**4. The judge reviews your case.**
If you’ve:
- completed the program cleanly,
- made good-faith efforts on court costs,
- and aren’t showing signs of gaming the system,

then they often use their discretion to **waive, forgive, or significantly reduce** the remaining balance. They can technically do this under **Rule 29** or similar state-level judicial discretion policies.

**5. You attend a short docket session.**
You’ll appear, say a few words if prompted (usually just a thank-you or update), and the judge will rule on the request—often with words like *“Let’s get this young man off our books,”* or *“He’s earned it.”*

---

### 🔧 Tips Before You Go

- Bring **proof of any payments made**, even if small or inconsistent. It shows good faith.
- Know your **current balance** if possible—ask the clerk in advance.
- Be ready to mention your **job or income** (even if it's modest—effort matters).
- If you were late on anything during the program, be ready to explain it briefly and show that you’ve corrected course.
