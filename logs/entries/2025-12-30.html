### ⚠️ Naltrexone Non-Responders: Why It Happens

**1. Biological Variability**
Your brain's opioid system may just not respond to naltrexone the same way as others. The drug blocks opioid receptors, which blunts the rewarding "kick" from alcohol or opioids. If your brain doesn't use those pathways heavily for reinforcement/cravings, the effect will be muted or nonexistent.

**2. Type of Substance Use**
Naltrexone tends to work best with alcohol and sometimes opioids. If your cravings are more psychological than biochemical—or if they stem from depression, habit, or trauma—it might not touch those.

**3. Genetic Differences**
Some people have variants in the **OPRM1 gene** (specifically A118G) that make them more or less responsive to naltrexone. It’s not something that’s routinely tested for, but it’s a known factor.

**4. Timing & Context**
It generally takes **5–7 days** to stabilize in your system, so you’re in range for full effect now. But the context matters—if cravings are situational (e.g., stress, social triggers, loneliness), the blunt effect of naltrexone may not be enough.
