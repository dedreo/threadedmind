Now, your side question — why the PSU migration:

---

### 🕰️ **A bit of history**
In the 1990s and early 2000s, PSUs sat **on top** of the case. That design came from the AT and early ATX standards:
- Power cords ran straight out the back near the monitor and peripherals.
- The PSU’s fan doubled as the **main exhaust**, pulling air from the CPU area and blowing it out.
- It saved money on extra fans — cases back then usually had *one fan total.*

It worked fine until CPUs and GPUs started spitting heat like hair dryers.

---

### 🔥 **Why it changed**
By the late 2000s:
1. **Thermals flipped.**
GPUs became the main heat source, not CPUs. Having the PSU above that hot zone meant it was sucking 60 °C air and aging fast.

2. **Dedicated airflow paths became standard.**
Bottom-mounted PSUs could draw **cool air directly from below** through a filtered intake, independent of GPU/CPU heat.

3. **Center of gravity + stability.**
Moving the PSU down lowered the tower’s weight distribution. Less wobble, especially for glass-sided, tall, or wide cases.

4. **Cable management and modular layouts.**
It freed the top of the motherboard area for radiators, extra fans, or AIO coolers — and allowed cleaner rear-chamber routing for cables (hence modern shrouds).

5. **Aesthetics evolved.**
Once clear side panels and RGB became the norm, hiding the ugly PSU and cable mess behind a shroud made builds look cleaner.

---

### ⚙️ **Bottom-mounted PSU = modern standard**
Now it’s rare to see any ATX case with a top PSU.
It’s:
- Cooler
- Quieter (since it’s isolated)
- Safer (less strain on components)
- Easier to build around modular power cables

The only exceptions are ultra-small form factor (ITX cube) or retro-styled cases where space dictates everything.

---

So yeah — the bottom placement wasn’t just aesthetic; it was a full thermal and ergonomic upgrade that aligned with the GPU arms race. Once GPUs started pulling more power than CPUs, the PSU got kicked downstairs for its own survival.

### 🔄 2. How desoldering wick (braid) actually works
That copper braid isn’t magic — it’s **capillary action + heat**:

1. **Flux:** the braid is usually pre-fluxed (or you add flux yourself). Flux lowers surface tension and helps molten solder flow.
2. **Heat:** you place the braid directly on the solder joint, then press the hot iron tip on top of the braid.
3. **Capillary action:** as the solder melts, the fine copper strands “wick” the liquid solder upward into the braid — same physics as a paper towel absorbing liquid.
4. **Lift:** once the section of braid is saturated (you’ll see it turn silver and stiffen), lift it away **while still hot** so it doesn’t stick.
5. **Trim:** cut off that used section, and move to a clean part of the braid for the next joint.

It’s ideal for:
- Clearing pads after removing a component.
- Fixing bridges between pins.
- Cleaning up excess solder from messy joints.

Hot tip (no pun):
- Use **fresh flux** and **light pressure** — pressing too hard will lift pads off the PCB.
- Use the smallest braid width that covers the joint; wider braid = slower heat transfer.

Here’s how it plays out technically:

---

### ⚙️ What they’re good at
- Small wires, light PCB joints, keyboard mods, repair on already-tinned pads.
- Quick field fixes powered by USB-C, PD, or a power bank.
- Learning how solder behaves — they’re precise, portable, and low-risk.

### 🔥 Where they fail
- Anything with **large thermal mass** (heatsinks, heavy leads, thick wires).
→ The tip dumps its limited heat into the joint faster than the heater can replace it, so the temp nosedives.
- **Continuous duty** (doing multiple joints back-to-back).
→ The internal temp sensor lags; heat recovery slows.
- **Durability.** Tiny heaters + cheap tips = burnout risk if run maxed too long.

Basically, they’re great for *finesse* but terrible for *force*.

---

If your use case stays in the “electronics tinkering” zone, one of those digital pens (especially the FNIRSI or Fanttik) will feel like an upgrade over the disposable engraver — precise, controlled, actually useful.

But if you expect to do “real” soldering sessions — e.g. multi-pin connectors, reflowing heatsinked components, or repeated jobs — you’ll want a **full station** with a larger heater cartridge and solid thermal recovery.

So yeah, they *are* the soldering equivalent of your old pen carver: fine for light detail, doomed if you push them past hobby intensity.
