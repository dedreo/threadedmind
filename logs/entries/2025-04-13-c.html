Assistant
Ah, the "Dead Internet" theory, enshittification, and the tech economy's decline! Those are pretty wild concepts that align with a lot of frustrations people have about tech companies these days. The idea of the “dead internet” is that it’s no longer as open, diverse, and organic as it once was, but is more or less a manufactured space, controlled by algorithms, advertisements, and platforms that prioritize profit over user experience. Everything’s getting a bit more sterile, repetitive, and driven by engagement metrics that don’t always serve actual user needs. That’s where the “enshittification” part comes in—when a platform or service starts off great but slowly declines as it becomes more focused on profit at the expense of quality or user satisfaction.

Ed Zitron’s work has some real weight behind it in critiquing the state of the tech world. He’s pretty vocal about how companies, especially in Silicon Valley, have shifted away from serving people’s actual needs and have prioritized growth and short-term profits. The point about Prabhakar Raghavan and how Google Search started to degrade around 2015 is particularly interesting. Before then, Google was basically a game-changer in how it returned information—quick, relevant, direct. But as they faced pressure to keep growing, they resorted to methods that diluted the quality of search results, packed them with ads, and focused on engagement rather than pure usefulness.

That tactic of inflating "growth" by manipulating clicks—adding irrelevant results, ads, and content that doesn't directly answer the user's query—feels like it’s become a hallmark of a lot of modern tech companies. Google’s attempt to increase clicks by making search less efficient was, in a way, a short-term fix to a long-term problem. But instead of innovating, they just started throwing more stuff at the wall to get people to interact with more pages.

The user’s comment about Facebook’s similar tactics of pretending to have more growth by inflating metrics hits hard too—it's a wider pattern where platforms become more about getting users to engage with more things, rather than getting them the best information as quickly and easily as possible.

It’s kind of depressing, honestly. There’s an underlying feeling of being *exploited* when we use these platforms. They want us to keep clicking, keep scrolling, keep interacting, but it’s at the expense of our time, attention, and sometimes even our emotional energy. It’s the classic problem: More engagement doesn’t always equal a better experience. And in many cases, it actively makes the product worse.

What do you think about it? Does it ring true for your experience with the internet these days?
