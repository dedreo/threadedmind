---

### 🈳 **Why Japanese Song Translation Gets Vague or Poetic**:

1. **Language structure** – Japanese leaves out a *lot* of subject/context that English demands. You might get:
→ *“Running through the night. Shining. Regret.”*
and translators have to guess:
→ *“He runs through the night, his regrets shining behind him.”*

2. **Cultural metaphor** – Phrases like “black and white” often imply more than color—it could mean emotional contrast, duality, life/death, social conflict, or racial/cultural commentary depending on context.

3. **Wordplay & kanji layering** – Rappers and lyricists *love* to stack meanings. A single word can have visual puns, slang meanings, historical references, and phonetic jokes **all at once**. Explaining that in English often breaks the poetry.

4. **Deliberate ambiguity** – A lot of Japanese lyrics are meant to *evoke*, not explain. The vibe is more important than a literal meaning.

The word you’re remembering might be:

### **“Yūgen” (幽玄)**
One of the deepest concepts in Japanese aesthetics. It’s that **profound, subtle awareness of the universe that triggers an emotional response too deep and mysterious for words**. Not quite longing, not quite awe—something in between, *evoked*, not explained.

But the one closest to what you're describing might be:

### **“Mono no aware” (物の哀れ)**
Often translated as *“the gentle sadness or wistfulness at the transience of things”*—but in a broader sense, it’s the ache for beauty that’s fleeting, or for something you *can feel slipping away*, even if you never really had it.

And then, *possibly the most specific one*:

### **“Natsukashii” (懐かしい)**
Which doesn’t just mean nostalgia—it means the **joyful recognition of something small that brings back a memory**, often of a time you didn’t even realize you cherished until now.
